--- title: Tour page-description: Why we appreciate Sketchpad
and feel its influence today. ---

1962 was a rich year for computer graphics. Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad was such a towering achievement that it is rightly considered "the definitive invention of interactive computer graphics", "computer aided design" (CAD) and "design simulation". However, for context, we need to understand that in many important areas in history, "ideas are often in the air", so we should not be surprised to also find the earliest great "video game" — SpaceWar by Steve Russell — and other display oriented CAD work by GM and others — happening the same year. In many ways this makes the actual accomplishments of Sketchpad at the same time even more unique.

Sketchpad not only helped to invent and motivate the future that followed it, including providing the very image of what "personal computing" could be like, but in many ways it also exceeded the imaginations of most who would try to do things with computers post- Sketchpad. It was not just "terrifically wonderful" 60+ years ago, but is still "absolutely wonderful" when encountered today.

The best way to appreciate Sketchpad is via historical movies, and physically interacting with modern day recreations of the system (for example, go to this website). The account here assumes it has been printed out and the reader is limited to a paper version.

Sketchpad started as a project to automate "mechanical drawing", which was previously done by hand using T-squares, triangles, paper and "engineering vellum", pencil and pen. This was intricate and painful, especially to fix the many mistakes the process gave rise to.

However, the initial emulated drawn lines on a CRT soon led to inventing useful processes that couldn't be done without using a computer. For example: providing dynamic rules ("this point should stick to this point or line", "this line should stay parallel to this line", "this point should always be 3 inches from this line"). Erasing, copying and duplicating could now be done easily. All editing operations could now be done on the display, and the results put to paper via a high-resolution plotter. (All the black on white drawings, old and new, in this article were made in Sketchpad and converted to plotted output.)

Something more powerful than just copying could be done: any drawing could serve as "master drawing" from which dynamically linked copies — instances — could be made, positioned, rotated and scaled. Changes to the master drawing would be reflected directly in the instances. This general idea and much of the technique is today referred to as "object- oriented design" and "object-oriented programming". It was an intrinsic power of Sketchpad, and was even used to make the text system (via making a master drawing for each character and creating instances of them when typing was done on the keyboard connected to the computer). Being able to use any drawing or combination of drawings as a master was more powerful and expressive than most OOP systems that followed.

Using a computer meant that *descriptions* of ideas were being manipulated, not concrete images on paper. One important implication was that instead of physical paper being a certain size and scale, the "virtual canvas" — i.e. the "world" — of Sketchpad could be enormous — e.g. a quarter mile on a side — and real-time computing could render the amount seen on the display via a "virtual window" that *clipped* and *scaled* the virtual canvas at will to seem as if a virtual camera was "zooming" and "panning". These effects were controlled in real-time by knobs the computer could sense.

At first, dynamic problem-solving was treated as isolated features for convenient drafting conventions, such as: a line should be a particular length, horizontal, vertical, or kept at an angle. It should be parallel to another line or connected in some way.

Soon this developed into a powerful general problem solver for non-linear relationships, and then into the realization that the drawings could often be treated as dynamic modelings of the *meanings* behind the drawings. For example, it is easy to make a drawing of a truss bridge in Sketchpad by drawing an element, then making more and merging them to make the entire bridge.

However, a Sketchpad line that represents an engineering "beam" can be made to act as it does in the physical world if we realize that a simple model for beams is that they are supposed to stay the same length.

Also imposing a "pull down" constraint (producing the -1286 in Figure 11) will create a situation where the best solution involves "stretching errors" that can be interpreted as stresses and strains on the beams — and these can be shown beam by beam using the text characters for numerals also made in Sketchpad.

In other words, we have not only designed a bridge using a computer (CAD), we have also been able to easily automatically SIMulate the design (CAD-SIM) to allow many quick and important experiments to be done with the design before it needs to be built. Many concepts were shown and/or invented so vividly that they became intrinsic properties of interactive computer graphics, including:

  • Drawing lines and arcs, dragging, constraining, copying, instantiation, labeling
  • Clipping window, world and screen
  • "Objects", instances, attachers, copies Solving simultaneous constraints, minimizing errors over whole systems, programming in terms of requirements ("what" vs. "hows").
  • CAD-SIM Simulating properties of designs
  • Representing property abstractions graphically, graphical user interface, etc.
  • (Philosophically) Underlying "universality" of thought and thinking about goals

Going far beyond just doing a more convenient version of an already existing medium — e.g. drafting system — to a qualitatively new kind of tool that could only be possible on a computer.

This fact helped some computerists think along the lines of "only possible on a computer" (rather than "just imitate for convenience") — this was a critical impact on the field as it was just starting out. "Sketchpad-like" thrilled the imagination, and was a beacon for the radical computer inventions of the 60s and 70s.
